# Helping people build a livelihood as AI changes work

Titans Forge policy recommendations | September 26, 2026

**People should be able to own the tools that make them productive.** Our preferred response to automation combines independent ownership, worker participation and protection during job transitions. The aim is to give people income, control and a lasting stake in the economy, rather than require them to rent access to every tool they need to earn a living.

These are the Forge's recommendations. They are not measured findings that robot ownership will replace a specified number of lost jobs. The ranking reflects implementation priorities, not estimated effect sizes. Income support starts immediately when someone loses work; its position below does not make it less urgent.

## 1 Make productive ownership accessible

Create voluntary routes for individuals and small firms to buy useful equipment, robots and local AI computing systems. Proposed tools include matched ownership grants, fairly priced equipment loans and transparent financing that transfers title. Prioritize displaced workers and viable local businesses that lack collateral. Funding should include installation, training and working capital, not just the purchase price.

Start with equipment that can perform a defined job reliably for paying customers. A cleaning business, groundskeeping service or small fabrication shop could test whether suitable automation improves its economics; these are candidate use cases, not promises about a particular robot. General-purpose robots should qualify only after their capabilities and costs are demonstrated in the intended setting.

An existing financing route is the [SBA Microloan program](https://www.sba.gov/loans/microloans/), which offers loans up to $50,000 through intermediaries and permits machinery and equipment purchases. Eligibility, underwriting and terms apply. This is not a robot grant, a promise of approval or necessarily enough funding for an entire operation. Additional ownership grants proposed here would require new funding.

## 2 Make ownership include practical control

Buying hardware should come with a usable right to operate, maintain, repair, resell and move the business to another supplier. Recommend clear software terms, replaceable components, accessible diagnostics, data export and local operation where technically feasible. Core purchased functionality should not disappear merely because an optional subscription ends. Security updates and safety requirements still matter.

Hardware title alone is insufficient if a vendor controls the machine, the customer list and the only way to obtain work. Examine recurring software, connectivity, maintenance and platform fees before purchase. An open model's license must actually permit the intended use; downloadable weights alone do not establish that right.

A short trial rental can help avoid buying unsuitable equipment. Leasing can sometimes be economical, but it should remain a choice rather than a permanent toll on a person's ability to work. Prefer arrangements with clear costs, an exit and a credible route to ownership when ownership is the objective.

## 3 Support shared ownership and worker buyouts

Where one person cannot afford a machine, workers or local businesses could jointly own it through a cooperative. Members need defined scheduling rights, voting rights, income allocation, repair reserves and a fair way to leave. A shared workshop can make equipment accessible without requiring each participant to borrow for an underused machine.

Help employees purchase viable local businesses from retiring owners and modernize them together. [USDA Cooperative Services](https://www.rd.usda.gov/programs-services/services/cooperative-services) describes employee purchases and worker cooperatives as an available business structure. That establishes a practical ownership route, not proof that every conversion succeeds. Other employee-ownership structures can suit some firms, but a financial stake and a voice in decisions are separate things and should be specified explicitly.

## 4 Help owners find customers and build local services

A machine without customers is a cost. Pair financing with paid pilot contracts, customer discovery, pricing help and introductions to buyers. Recommend smaller, accessible procurement lots where appropriate so qualified local firms can compete. Keep quality, safety and fair competition requirements intact.

Build local work around installation, maintenance, repair, supervision and useful services enabled by the equipment. Support shared workshops and technical colleges as places to test tools and learn their limits. Measure the net effect on nearby firms too: shifting the same customers between businesses is not automatically job creation.

## 5 Tie paid training to real work

Provide paid, task-specific training linked to an employer, a service contract or an ownership plan. Cover equipment operation, verification, maintenance, sales and basic business finance where relevant. Include apprenticeships and accessible routes for people without advanced degrees. Childcare, transport and training-time income determine whether many workers can participate.

Measure placement, earnings recovery and durable business income rather than course completions alone. The [OECD's work on AI and skills](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2023/07/oecd-employment-outlook-2023_904bcef3/full-report/skill-needs-and-policies-in-the-age-of-artificial-intelligence_fe530fbf.html) provides background on adapting training to changing work. The specific ownership-linked package here is a Forge proposal.

## 6 Protect income during the transition

Strengthen access to unemployment support, continued health coverage, job-search help and retraining support. Consider time-limited wage insurance for people accepting lower-paid work, with evaluation of whether it improves recovery or subsidizes persistently low wages. Benefit transitions should not create abrupt penalties for a worker testing a small business.

Nobody should have to take on equipment debt to qualify for help. People who cannot or do not want to operate a business need credible employment and income options. Ownership should widen their choices. It is not a substitute for a safety net.

## 7 Share productivity gains within existing firms

Encourage meaningful employee ownership, transparent profit sharing and worker participation in how automation changes jobs. Consider shorter hours with protected pay where realized productivity gains can support them. Avoid promising that technology alone can fund every arrangement.

Ownership plans should have understandable rights, fair valuations and protections against excessive concentration of workers' savings in the same business that pays their wages. Profit sharing can distribute income, but it should not be mislabeled as ownership. Training and redeployment should be considered before avoidable layoffs.

## 8 Keep entry and competition open

Support interoperability, repair access and the ability to change suppliers without losing business records. Public support for automation should include opportunities for smaller firms and ownership by workers, rather than default exclusively to large operators. Apply competition policy to exclusionary conduct on its evidence; company size alone does not establish misconduct.

If labor demand falls broadly despite these measures, test wider income-sharing options, including a publicly accountable capital fund with citizen dividends. Such a proposal needs a funding source, governance rules and evidence about who gains and who pays. Do not present an unfunded dividend as a solution already available.

## Test the economics before scaling

For each candidate business, estimate revenue at realistic paid utilization and subtract materials, energy, software, repairs, insurance, finance costs and a replacement reserve. Count human supervision, administration and sales time at a fair wage. Test a low-demand period and a prolonged equipment outage. Owning a robot does not create guaranteed passive income.

A proposed local pilot would first identify paying demand and suitable equipment, then trial the service before committing to larger purchases. Evaluate earnings after all costs, owner control, debt burden, downtime, safety, repeat customers and worker transitions at 3, 6 and 12 months. Compare with ordinary small-business and retraining support where feasible. Include unsuccessful firms and spillovers on competing local workers. The goal is durable income and independence, not the number of robots distributed.
